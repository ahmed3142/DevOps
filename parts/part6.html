Part 6

Cloud, Cost and Leading the Change

Lectures 13, 14 and 15 — cloud environment management, FinOps, and the adoption roadmap.

6.1 Cloud environment management

Environments are separated into distinct cloud accounts rather than distinguished by naming convention within one. This bounds the blast radius of a mistake, makes least-privilege access straightforward, and produces clean cost attribution for free, since the bill arrives already divided by environment. Organisation-level guardrails prevent whole classes of error — unapproved regions, disabled audit logging — without anyone having to remember the rule.

6.2 FinOps: making cost a design input

The scenario's climbing bill with no clear owner is not primarily a technical problem. Cost is decided by engineers making architectural choices and reviewed by nobody, because the people spending the money never see the invoice. FinOps closes that loop in three stages. Inform: tagging is mandatory and enforced by the policy checks in Part 5, so every resource declares owner, environment, service and cost centre, and dashboards are visible to engineers rather than only to finance. The headline figure is deliberately a unit measure — infrastructure cost per tenant per month — because rising absolute spend is not a problem if revenue rises faster, whereas rising cost per tenant means the business becomes less profitable as it grows.

Optimise: the immediate wins are unglamorous and large — right-sizing instances provisioned for a peak that never arrives, autoscaling to real demand, committed-use discounts for the stable baseline, spot capacity for pipeline runners, retention tiers, and shutting non-production environments outside working hours, which alone typically removes two-thirds of their cost. Operate: budgets and anomaly alerts route to the owning team rather than a monthly finance review, and a cost-estimation step in each pull request shows the monetary delta of an infrastructure change before it merges — making cost a design-time input in the way test results already are.

Trade-off honesty

Optimisation is itself an investment, and at Nimbus's size engineering time costs more than infrastructure. Spending three weeks of engineering effort to recover a few hundred euro a month is a poor trade; work is prioritised by return, targeting unowned waste — idle non-production capacity, oversized instances — rather than architectural rewrites that buy efficiency at the cost of delivery speed. Above all, a named human owns the number. The scenario's real defect is the absent owner, and no tool substitutes for one.

6.3 Leading the change

The technical plan is the straightforward half. This transformation succeeds or fails on whether seven people change how they work, and the sequencing must respect that. It therefore starts with pain the team already feels rather than with an abstract maturity model: automating the release everyone dreads buys credibility for everything that follows, whereas opening with policy enforcement would be experienced as bureaucracy imposed on people who never asked for it.

The most important stakeholder is Sam. The single expert can veto this programme, and their standing currently derives from being indispensable — so a plan that quietly removes that status will be resisted whatever its engineering merits. The framing must be a promotion rather than a dilution: Sam becomes owner and architect of the new platform, with the pipeline and infrastructure code as their product. Knowledge transfer then becomes structural rather than aspirational, through pairing, mandatory review and written architecture decision records, and it is measured — the number of engineers who can independently deploy and recover production is tracked as an explicit metric, starting at one.

Different audiences need the same programme in their own terms: founders and investors hear reduced delivery risk and improving cost per tenant, sales hears fewer incidents, engineers hear no weekend releases. Presenting identical work in a single vocabulary is how such programmes lose their sponsor.

Technical debt gets a standing allocation — roughly a fifth of each iteration — rather than a remediation sprint that is always displaced by a customer commitment, recorded in a register with its ongoing cost stated, because "we should tidy this up" loses every prioritisation argument while "this costs six engineer-hours a month" wins many. Decision records preserve the reasoning, which is the part that leaves with a departing employee. Finally, "you build it, you run it" carries a caveat usually omitted: it works only when the team also holds the authority to change what they run and the time to do it. Without both it is merely extra pager duty.

The order is deliberate. Measurement precedes change because an improvement that cannot be evidenced cannot be funded; repeatability precedes safety because automated rollback is meaningless without a reproducible artefact to roll back to; and security and cost follow rather than lead, not because they matter less but because gates applied to a still-manual process are merely obstacles. The principal risks are change fatigue, managed by limiting the programme to one significant change at a time, and automation theatre — measuring pipelines built rather than outcomes improved.

6.5 Measuring success

Progress is reviewed monthly against the baseline in Table 1: the four delivery metrics, objective attainment and error-budget consumption, cost per tenant, the number of engineers able to deploy and recover production, and engineering time lost to unplanned interruption. The twelve-month goal is daily deployment, lead time under a day, change failure below ten per cent and restoration within thirty minutes — at a lower cost per customer than today.

That combination is the point. The assumption this strategy is designed to disprove is that speed must be traded against stability. The research underpinning the DORA metrics finds the opposite: the practices that make delivery fast — small batches, automated verification, reproducible environments, fast feedback — are the same ones that make it safe, because a small, tested, reversible change is both quicker to ship and easier to diagnose when it fails.
