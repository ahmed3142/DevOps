Part Six

Cloud, Cost and Leading the Change

Cloud environment management, FinOps, the adoption roadmap and how success is measured.

6.1 Cloud environment management

Environments are separated into distinct cloud accounts rather than distinguished by naming convention inside one account. This bounds the blast radius of a mistake, makes least-privilege access straightforward, and produces clean cost attribution as a side effect, since the invoice arrives already divided by environment. Organisation-level guardrails prevent whole classes of error, such as deploying into unapproved regions or disabling audit logging, without anyone having to remember the rule.

6.2 Cost as a design input

The climbing bill with no clear owner is not primarily a technical problem. Cost is determined by engineers making architectural choices and reviewed by nobody, because the people spending the money never see the invoice. FinOps closes that loop in three stages.

Inform. Tagging is mandatory and enforced by the policy checks in Part 5, so every resource declares an owner, environment, service and cost centre, and dashboards are visible to engineers rather than only to finance. The headline figure is a unit measure, cost per tenant per month, because rising total spend is fine if revenue rises faster, whereas rising cost per tenant means the business becomes less profitable as it grows.

Optimise. The first wins are unglamorous and large: right-sizing instances provisioned for a peak that never arrives, autoscaling to real demand, committed-use discounts for the stable baseline, spot capacity for pipeline runners, retention tiers, and shutting non-production environments outside working hours, which alone removes about two-thirds of their cost.

Operate. Budgets and anomaly alerts route to the owning team rather than a monthly finance review, and a cost estimate in each pull request shows the effect of an infrastructure change before it merges, making cost a design-time input in the way test results already are.

Optimisation is itself an investment.

At Nimbus's size engineering time costs more than infrastructure, so spending three weeks of effort to recover a few hundred euro a month is a poor trade. Work is prioritised by return, starting with unowned waste such as idle non-production capacity and oversized instances, rather than architectural rewrites that buy efficiency at the cost of delivery speed. Above all, one named person owns the number; the defect in the scenario is an absent owner, and no tool substitutes for one.

6.3 Leading the change

The technical plan is the easier half. This programme succeeds or fails on whether seven people change how they work, so it starts with a problem the team already feels. Automating the release everyone dreads earns credibility for what follows, whereas opening with policy enforcement reads as bureaucracy imposed on people who did not ask for it.

The most important stakeholder is Sam. The single expert can block this programme, and their standing in the company currently derives from being indispensable, so a plan that quietly removes that standing will be resisted whatever its engineering merits. The framing has to be a promotion rather than a dilution: Sam becomes owner and architect of the new platform, with the pipeline and the infrastructure code as their product. Knowledge transfer then becomes structural rather than aspirational, through pairing, mandatory review and written decision records, and it is measured. The number of engineers who can independently deploy and recover production is tracked as a metric in its own right, and it starts at one.

The same programme needs describing differently to different audiences. Founders and investors hear reduced delivery risk and improving cost per tenant, sales hears fewer incidents, engineers hear no weekend releases. The work is identical, and presenting it in a single vocabulary is how programmes like this lose their sponsor.

Technical debt receives a standing allocation of roughly a fifth of each iteration rather than a remediation sprint that is always displaced by a customer commitment, recorded in a register with its monthly cost stated, because a request to tidy something up loses every prioritisation argument while a cost of six engineer-hours a month wins many. The principle that a team runs what it builds carries a condition usually omitted: it works only where the team also holds the authority to change what it runs and the time to do so. Without both, it is on-call duty under another name.

The order is deliberate. Measurement precedes change because improvement that cannot be evidenced cannot be funded, and repeatability precedes safety because automated rollback is meaningless without a reproducible artefact to roll back to. Security and cost follow rather than lead, because gates applied to a still-manual process are obstacles rather than controls. The main risks are change fatigue, managed by limiting the programme to one significant change at a time, and automation theatre, where pipelines built are mistaken for outcomes improved.

6.5 Measuring success

Progress is reviewed monthly against Table 1: the four delivery metrics, objective attainment, error-budget consumption, cost per tenant, the number of engineers able to deploy and recover production, and engineering time lost to unplanned interruption. The twelve-month goal is daily deployment, lead time under a day, change failure below ten per cent and restoration within thirty minutes, at a lower cost per customer than today.

That combination is the argument of this report. The assumption at Nimbus is that speed must be traded against stability, and the DORA research finds the opposite. The practices that make delivery fast, namely small batches, automated verification, reproducible environments and fast feedback, are the same ones that make it safe, because a small, tested, reversible change is both quicker to ship and easier to diagnose when it fails.
