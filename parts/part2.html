Part 2

Version Control and Collaborative Workflow

Lecture 2 — branching strategy, code review, and traceability from ticket to running version.

2.1 The chosen strategy

Nimbus will adopt trunk-based development with short-lived branches. Every change is cut from main, lives on a branch for no more than two days, and returns through a pull request requiring a passing pipeline and one approving review. main is protected: no direct pushes, no force-pushes, no merge without a green build. This is the workflow demonstrated in the supporting repository, where four feature branches were merged into a protected trunk and released as an annotated tag.

2.2 Why not the alternatives

GitFlow is the obvious incumbent choice and the wrong one here. Its develop, release and hotfix branches earn their keep when a vendor ships versioned software to customers who choose when to upgrade. Nimbus runs one production instance shared by every customer, so those branches buy no isolation while formalising the very long-lived-branch behaviour that produces its seventeen-day lead time — institutionalising the disease as a process.

Committing directly to trunk without review — the purest form of trunk-based development — is equally unsuitable, for a cultural rather than technical reason. Nimbus's central risk is that one engineer holds all production knowledge, and a pull request is the cheapest knowledge-transfer mechanism available: it forces a second person to read every change and records why it was made. Review here is not a gate bolted on out of distrust; it is deliberate redundancy in the team's understanding.

Design decision — batch size is the real lever

The branching model matters mainly because it caps batch size. Twelve small merges a week carry less risk than one large merge a month: each contains fewer changes, is easier to review honestly, is faster to diagnose when it fails, and is trivial to revert. Small batches improve lead time and change-failure rate simultaneously — which is why those metrics move together rather than trade off.

2.3 Conventions, protection and release tagging

Commits follow the Conventional Commits specification — valuable not for tidiness but for machine readability, since a structured history lets changelogs and version numbers be generated rather than negotiated. Merge commits are retained deliberately: a merge groups a feature's commits into one revertible unit, whereas squashing discards the intermediate steps that make a bisect meaningful. Releases are annotated semantic-version tags on main, so a version number identifies one commit, one image and one set of passing tests.

2.4 Traceability

An unbroken chain connects a business request to a running process. The ticket identifier appears in the branch name, carries into the commit messages, is referenced by the pull request recording review and approval, is captured by the release tag, is baked into the image digest, and is finally reported by the service itself — the /api/health endpoint in the supporting repository returns the version it is executing. Any environment can therefore be traced to the commit that produced it without asking anyone.

That chain turns version control from a backup mechanism into the organisation's audit trail: it answers what changed, when and who approved it in seconds rather than through forensic archaeology across Slack history, and at no extra cost produces the evidence a security questionnaire will later demand.
