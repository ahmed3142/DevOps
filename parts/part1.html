Part 1

Current-State Analysis and the Case for DevOps

Lectures 1 and 3 — DevOps culture, value flow, and the measurement baseline.

1.1 The organisation and how work flows

Nimbus Ltd. is a fifteen-person start-up whose product, Nimbus Notes, is a subscription knowledge base used by around four hundred small-business teams. Seven engineers build it, on two Amazon EC2 instances configured by hand over SSH at founding and edited in place ever since. The process that suited three people in one room is now the constraint on a company preparing for a funding round and a doubling of its engineering team.

A change begins as a ticket, is developed on a branch that lives two to three weeks, is reviewed informally if at all, is checked against a manual regression spreadsheet, and reaches production only when Sam — the founding engineer — runs deploy.sh from a laptop. Figure 1 maps that value stream: seventeen calendar days from commitment to production, of which roughly two and a half involve anyone touching the change. Flow efficiency is about fifteen per cent.

Why the framing matters

If eighty-five per cent of lead time is queueing rather than working, hiring more developers will lengthen the queues rather than shorten them. The constraint is the process, not the capacity — which is what justifies investing in delivery infrastructure before headcount.

1.2 Waste, handoffs and silos

Reading the same value stream through the seven wastes of Lean turns complaints into a diagnosis. Partially done work accumulates in branches unmerged for weeks. Handoffs occur three times per change — developer, tester, deployer — each discarding context that is then rebuilt by conversation. Waiting is the largest category, because every release queues behind one person's calendar. Defects escape at one corrective deploy per four releases. Task switching costs twice over, since the engineer interrupted by deployments is the one whose own work then stalls. Relearning happens in every outage, as production knowledge is re-derived under pressure without runbooks, and over-processing appears as a manual checklist re-executed identically each release.

The silo here is not the classic wall between development and operations; the company is too small to have either. It is a knowledge silo: one engineer understands how production is wired, which makes that person the bottleneck, the single point of failure, and the one who can never take a holiday. The risk is a bus factor of one, and it appears on no project plan.

1.3 Why DevOps, and where CALMS falls short

CALMS describes what a mature organisation looks like: a Culture of shared ownership rather than blame, Automation of repetitive error-prone work, Lean flow in small batches, Measurement of outcomes, and Sharing of knowledge across roles. Nimbus scores poorly on all five, and the model usefully explains why the symptoms are connected rather than coincidental.

CALMS is nevertheless a description, not a plan. It supplies no sequence, and a team reading it naively begins with Automation because tooling is the most tangible letter — automating a process nobody has measured or understands. It is also silent on cost and security, which is why FinOps and DevSecOps had to be appended to the model rather than derived from it. At Nimbus the correct entry points are Measurement, because a start-up that cannot evidence improvement cannot defend the investment producing it, and Sharing, because the bus factor is the acute risk. Automation follows from those two rather than preceding them.
